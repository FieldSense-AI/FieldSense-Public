# The problem

## Fields are not uniform. Treatment usually is.

Soil varies over a few metres. Drainage differs across a slope, organic matter
collects in some corners and not others, and last season's traffic compacted
strips that have not recovered. A field is a patchwork.

Fertiliser is applied as though it were not. One rate, everywhere.

The result is failure at both ends of the same field:

- **The underfed half** does not reach its yield potential, no matter what was spent.
- **The overfed half** is the expensive half. Surplus nitrogen leaches into groundwater
  rather than into the crop, salts accumulate, and the following season needs *more*
  input to achieve the same result.

Uniform treatment does not average out. It compounds.

## Why laboratory testing does not close the gap

Laboratory soil analysis is accurate, and it is the right instrument for
establishing ground truth. It is the wrong instrument for a decision that has to
be made this week, for two reasons.

**Turnaround.** One to two weeks between taking a sample and reading a result.
The window in which the answer could have changed what you did has usually closed.

**Cost per sample.** Because each sample costs money and effort, a field is
typically represented by two or three of them. Two or three points cannot describe
a patchwork. They produce one number for the whole field — which is exactly the
uniform picture that caused the problem.

Sampling density is the thing that matters, and it is the thing the economics
of laboratory testing make impossible.

## What is actually needed

An instrument that is cheap enough per reading that density stops being the
constraint, fast enough that the answer arrives while the decision is still open,
and independent enough to work where the field is.

That means:

- **Many points per field**, not two or three.
- **An answer in the field**, not a fortnight later.
- **No connectivity requirement.** Rural fields do not reliably have a network,
  and a tool that fails without one fails exactly where it is needed.
- **Output a person can act on** — which patch, and what about it — rather than a
  table of numbers requiring an agronomist to interpret.

FieldSense is an attempt at that instrument.

## What this does not claim

FieldSense does not replace laboratory analysis, and it is not trying to.

A laboratory measures absolute values against certified standards. FieldSense
measures *relative variation across a field* with a portable probe, quickly and
at many points. Those are different jobs. The honest framing is that FieldSense
tells you **where in the field to look and how the patches differ** — and a
laboratory tells you precisely what is there.
